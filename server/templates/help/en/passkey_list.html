What a passkey is

A passkey replaces the password with the device: when signing in, the device asks for face, finger or PIN, and the browser confirms to the application that it is you. There is nothing to type and nothing anyone could guess or intercept. The password stays valid alongside, say for a foreign device.

Creating one

A passkey belongs to the device it is created on. With Apple and Google it travels to the other devices of the same account. Whoever works with several devices creates one per device and gives it a name to tell them apart. Signing in then needs no username: the button Sign in with passkey is enough.

Before creating one, the page asks for your password, and only then does the device ask for face, finger or PIN. That way nobody creates a passkey who only has your device in hand or finds a session left open. A wrong password counts like a failed attempt at signing in. After too many the account is temporarily locked from here, signing in included.

If your account has no password, you confirm with one of your passkeys instead. After Confirm with passkey the device asks for face, finger or PIN, and only the passkeys of your account are offered. The confirmation holds for two minutes. If the dialog breaks off while creating the passkey, simply try again within that time. Without a password and without a passkey there is nothing to confirm with. Then an administrator can set a password for you.

Where it is kept

Where the passkey is stored is up to the device: in Windows Hello, in the iCloud Keychain, in Google Password Manager or in another password manager. A password manager in the browser usually catches the request and offers to store the passkey itself. Whoever would rather keep it on the device picks the other option in its window or switches off saving passkeys there. If you leave the name field empty, the passkey is named after its store, say “Windows Hello” or “iCloud Keychain”. That way you see where it ended up.

Removing one

A device that got lost loses its access here: removing takes the passkey out of the application and signs your account out everywhere. On the website that applies at once, in the counting app within an hour at the latest. You stay signed in only where you remove the passkey. Whatever the counting app has not sent yet stays and goes out after the next sign-in. On the device the passkey stays and is simply unknown then. There it can be deleted in the device's settings.

Before removing a passkey the page asks for your password, as before creating one. Otherwise somebody who finds a session left open could remove your passkeys and sign you out everywhere, while the session they found stays signed in. A wrong password counts like a failed attempt at signing in. If your account has no password, you first confirm at the top of the page with one of your passkeys. The confirmation holds for two minutes, for several passkeys in a row too.

Without a password your last passkey is the only way into your account, and a new one is confirmed with one you have. That is why the page asks once more before the last one is removed. Once it is gone, you sign in again only after an administrator has set a password for you. So create the new one first and remove the old one afterwards.

In the counting app

The counting app has the same passkeys in its account menu. Whoever counts on the phone creates the passkey there, because the installed app on the iPhone has a storage of its own, and a passkey from the browser does not reach it. The app asks for the password before creating and before removing one as well, and an account without a password confirms there with one of its passkeys too. Signing in to the app then only takes “Sign in with passkey”.

What comes with it

Passkeys are bound to the application's address. If it moves to another domain, the old ones no longer apply, and every device creates a new one. An administrator can remove passkeys in the administration, say when somebody no longer has their device. Then too the account is signed out everywhere afterwards.
